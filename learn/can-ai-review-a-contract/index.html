Can AI review a contract?

By Jordy Backes Published 2026-08-31 Facts checked 2026-08-30

Answer

Yes, and the useful form is a redline in Word rather than a chat answer. Given the contract and the firm's written positions, the review in the build video behind this page came back in about 8 minutes with a ranked risk memo and the NDA redlined: 17 clauses reviewed, 12 redlines, 2 red flags, 2 items for a lawyer.

[ The short version ]

  • AI reviews a contract well when it reviews against something: the firm's positions, written down once in plain English, each with a severity.
  • The useful output is the contract itself redlined in Word with tracked changes, plus a memo that names the position behind every edit.
  • Where no written position covers a clause, the recorded build made no edit and flagged the clause for a lawyer instead of inventing a standard.
  • In the build video: 17 clauses reviewed, 12 redlines made, 2 red flags, 2 items needing a lawyer's input, in about 8 minutes.
  • Review-ready, never send-ready: a licensed attorney accepts or rejects every change, the same way an associate's redline gets reviewed.

AI can read a contract and produce comments about it, and so can a summary tool. A law firm’s question is narrower: can it produce a redline a lawyer would accept, with the firm’s own positions underneath every edit and the clauses it could not verify honestly left alone. It can, and the counts on this page come from two build videos recorded start to finish, each on a synthetic NDA: fake firm, fake clients, real workflows.

What decides the answer: the positions file

A contract review is a comparison, and the quality of the review is decided by what the contract is compared against. Ask a chat window to review an NDA and it compares the NDA against generic best practice, then hands back advice you retype into the document yourself. Give it a file that says what this firm accepts and what it pushes back on, and the edits follow the firm’s own terms.

In the recorded build that file carried 8 positions in plain English, each with a severity. The severity decides the color: a high severity position produces the red findings, a medium one the yellow. Two of the positions are visible in the redline itself. Section 6 of the NDA carried a seven year confidentiality term, and the firm’s standard is three, so the term was struck to three. Section 7 gave five business days to destroy every copy, and the position requires ten plus a carve-out for routine backups, so that is what the tracked change wrote in.

AI reviews a contract well exactly when it is told, in writing, what the firm would say.

The workflow that produced a review-ready redline

The build video runs as five moves, and the same five usually transfer to any firm that has its positions written down.

  1. 01

    Write the firm's positions down first

    One position per clause type, in plain English: what is acceptable, what to push back on, and a severity that says how hard to flag it. In the recorded build the file carried 8 positions, and every later edit traced back to one of them. A review against generic best practice produces generic edits; a review against this file produces the firm's edits.

    Result: A standard the review can be checked against, in the firm's own words.

  2. 02

    Put the contract and the positions in one folder

    The desktop app's Code tab works inside one folder on your computer. Drop the contract in as a Word file; a .docx is what makes tracked changes possible later. If the matter lives in Clio or another document system, export the file first and drop it in the same way.

    Result: The contract and the standard sit side by side where the review runs.

  3. 03

    Run the review with one typed command

    In the recorded build the whole instruction was /redline-contract, typed into the chat. Every clause gets checked against the positions file: green passes, yellow needs a look, red is the highest priority. Where a clause has no written position behind it, the review makes no edit and flags it.

    Result: About 8 minutes later on the recorded NDA: a ranked risk memo and the redlined contract, saved beside the original.

  4. 04

    Read the memo before the redline

    The memo ranks every finding from red to green and gives three things per clause: what it says, which position it violates, and the exact edit made. Below the ranked cards sits the list of items it could not verify, each with what to request from the other side. In the recorded run there were two, a missing exhibit and an unprovided security standard, and neither clause received an edit.

    Result: You know which clauses nobody edited, and why, before you open Word.

  5. 05

    Open the redline in Word and trace two changes

    The markings are native tracked changes, handled with the review tools the firm already uses. Pick two edits and follow them to their positions. In the recorded NDA, section 6 struck a seven year confidentiality term to three years because the firm's standard is three, and section 7 widened five business days to destroy every copy to ten, with a carve-out for routine backups and one compliance copy. Then accept or reject each change, one by one.

    Result: A reviewed redline with the memo as its paper trail, approved by a licensed attorney before it leaves the firm.

What to verify before it leaves

Each item below names where the recorded workflow handles it.

[ Checklist ]

Verify these before any AI redline leaves the firm

  • Every edit against its position. Open the memo card for each change and read the position it cites. An edit with no position behind it is a guess, and a guess gets rejected. Step 4 reads the memo card by card.

  • The clauses with no edit. The clauses flagged without an edit are the ones it could not verify. Each needs a lawyer's own read, and usually a request to the other side. Step 4 lists them at the bottom of the memo.

  • Defined terms and cross-references. A struck sentence can orphan a defined term or a section number somewhere else in the document. Search for both after accepting changes.

  • The business decisions. Where the positions file says business decision, the review flags the clause and leaves it. That call is the firm's, and once made it belongs in the positions file for next time.

  • Author and date on every change. Click a tracked change and read its card. The firm's name and the run's date belong on every one. Step 5 shows the card.

AI changes nothing about who signs. The redline is review-ready, never send-ready, and a licensed attorney approves everything before it leaves the firm.

The no-setup path, if you want to try it today

The recorded build runs on one installed skill and one positions file, and the whole folder is a free download on its build page, run in the desktop app on a paid plan. For a first run without any setup, this prompt reviews pasted contract text against pasted positions and refuses to edit what no position covers.

This prompt is narrower than the build on purpose: the replacement wording comes back in the chat, and you retype it into the document yourself. The build on its page puts the changes into Word as tracked changes, and the positions live in one file the firm edits once. Once the same review runs more than twice, that file is the next step: save it with the review instruction as a skill file in the folder, the way the build page does, and the same positions apply the same way on almost every run.

Honest limits

Three limits from the recorded build, stated plainly:

  • It needs Word going in. Tracked changes need a .docx contract; a PDF or a plain text contract still gets the memo and the list of edits, and no redlined Word document.
  • It reviews only against what is written. A position the firm never wrote down cannot produce an edit, and the positions file shipped with the build video covers NDAs only. Other contract types start with someone at the firm writing the positions first.
  • It cannot judge the deal. Whether this counterparty will accept the pushback, and whether the clause is worth the fight, stays with the lawyer. The recorded build flags those clauses and leaves them.

06 / FAQ

The questions, answered

Can AI review a contract for free?

A free chat can read a contract and comment on it. The useful form needs two things a chat window does not have: the firm's written positions and the contract as a Word file, so the edits come back as tracked changes. The review folder from the recorded build is a free download; the app it runs in needs a paid plan.

Can a law firm rely on an AI redline?

The way it relies on an associate's redline: after review. Every tracked change is accepted or rejected by a lawyer, the memo names the position behind each edit, and the clauses it could not verify carry no edit at all. What leaves the firm is the lawyer's redline, whoever produced the first pass.

How long does an AI contract review take?

About 8 minutes on the recorded 12 section NDA, from the typed command to the two files. In the second build video, an NDA forwarded by email came back in the same thread about four minutes later with 12 clauses reviewed and 3 red flags. The real work is writing the positions file the first time.

What does AI get wrong in contract review?

The documented failure is inventing a standard where none is written. In the model comparison on the build page, one model accepted a venue objection waiver as satisfied where no position covered it, while the other made no edit and flagged the clause for attorney judgment. The fix is one rule written into the positions file: no position, no edit.

Does it work on contracts other than NDAs?

The method is the same for any contract type: positions written first, then the review against them. The positions file shipped with the build video covers NDAs only, so for a services agreement or a lease someone at the firm writes those positions before the first run. That is real work, and it is what makes the edits the firm's.
